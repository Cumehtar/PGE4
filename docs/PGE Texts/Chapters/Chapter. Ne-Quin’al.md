You may expect this chapter to lead you through a measured stroll across the plains and dunes of [[docs/Wiki/Polities/Ne-Quin’al|Ne-Quin’al]], marking the important cities, telling you where the fabric is cheap, or where demand for bonemold is high. Let me disappoint you, dear reader, and ask you to bear with me, for I believe that before you understand the land, you should understand the people.

And to understand the people, you should understand their tongue - or at the very least admit your lack of understanding humbly. You may know that the language of the Khajiit is called Ta’agra, and that is a valuable first step. But what is important to know is that it is a family of languages and dialects, and knowing some of them doesn’t grant you proficiency in the others.

What is even more important, Ta’agra has a specific simplified mode of speaking, used with children, the dim-witted and foreigners - the pup-speech, so to speak. If you are reminded of the specific quirks of Khajiit speech - always speaking in the third person, avoiding complicated structures and long sentences - it is them falling into a pup-speech habit for simplicity. True Ta’agra dialects, as used among themselves, are among the most complicated languages of Tamriel, and the dialect of the Khajiiti heartlands - the arid plains of Ne-Quin’al - is the most peculiar of them all.

There is no way to simply say ‘I will help you’ in Ta’agra - except by saying ‘khajiit helps you’ in pup-speech. In proper speech between equals, each word comes marked with a suffix that describes the speaker and his furstock, his attitude to the act or promise he is making - somewhat like a verbal mood with the marked exception of Ta’agra having upwards of 32 moods, - his attitude to the interlocutor, their respective ranks, and the exact one of the 16 phases of the two moons the speech act refers to. What is more, most of those suffixes are distinguished from each other only by a vowel tone and length, often too minutely for any but the most sensitive hearing to catch. Thus ‘I-as-an-equal will-forever-swear help-support-gladly you-my-dearest-friend’ sounds almost the same as ‘I-defeated will-under-duress help-until-next-moon you-sworn-enemy’. Consider that this may be where the Khajiit reputation for being untrustworthy comes from, especially if the promise was made in pup-speech, which doesn’t allow for such nuance.

It also explains another custom you may have seen, when two khajiit square off and sing-song the same phrase back and forth. It is not a mind-numbing repetition in fact, it is a game that demands the most of mental agility, for they change the phrase spoken just a bit, and the purpose of the game is to go as long as possible while still making sense. The best practitioners last for hours.

Hopefully, after this introduction, you are starting to understand what to expect when you embark on this journey. One more warning is in order, however. Many traders visiting Ne Quin-al thought that the local folk would be more hospitable to those of the same race, so they brought other Khajiit as translators. The sad and mutually frustrating fact is that cat-folk of other lands, especially the khajiit of Resdayn for reasons I don’t need to elaborate, don’t speak Ta’agra beyond pup-speech themselves. So while a flesh-faced foreigner meowing pitifully in simple monosyllabic words will be seen as adorable, and even a bit admirable, hearing the same from a full-grown cat is insulting.

Thus forewarned, let us step on the warm sands, but remember to avoid cities. In the open land, every piece of it is a range that belongs to a clan or a family, and that clan is headed by a watchful grandmother. A foreigner, a visitor, a trader - they are a guest of the range by a custom stronger than law until proven otherwise. But the city belongs to nobody, and no custom rules there. A visitor is a mark, a target, someone to swindle, fleece, pickpocket, and rob. “Wait,” you will ask, “but what about the grand temple festivals of [[docs/Wiki/Locations/Dunei|Dunei]]?” But that is exactly the answer - the pilgrims flood the cities during festivals. There Mane visits them, towering on his floating platform. That is when the city becomes warm, welcoming, and safe, like a temple during a service. But ask yourself about the character of those who continue living on the temple steps when the procession leaves, having nowhere better to go.

So better keep your steps away from the cities, and choose your way. One way to go is north-west, towards the great [[Colovian Steppe]]. The ranges grow bigger there, and you may notice more people of other races walking among the clans. There are complicated networks of adopted, symbolic, and inherited kinship stretching across the steppes all the way to the nomads of the Yokedate, the greatest known being the clans of [[docs/Wiki/Organizations/Sacred/Mother-Navigators|Mother-Navigators]] and Legions of Water.

Since those kin are considered khajiit, and since you need to know your own furstock to speak Ta’agra properly, there are multiple theories on how to determine it for the folk that only ever have one shape. They range from simply using the lunar phase to complicated layered calendars that calculate the cross-movements of the heavens - the star signs, the planetary movements, the distance of the Serpent from the Sun. It is a common knowledge that city charlatans can produce an astronomical calendar for you that matches you to the furstock you want.

Keep away from the south-west. The line on the map may be neat, but in actuality the ranges grow, contract, and shift with moons and seasons, and you don’t want to find yourself in the jungle-covered lands of [[Pa'alatiin]] without proper paperwork. The relationship between the two khajiit kingdoms has always been strained, and the current quiet civil war and its reasons deserve a separate essay I can’t in good conscience attempt.

If you go south-east, though, over smaller, ever more insular ranges, you will reach the port of [[docs/Wiki/Locations/Leyawiin|Leyawiin]]. As the cities of Ne-Quin’al go, it is the safest for outsiders. It is the only interface the kingdom has between the Sand Clans and the Water Clans - and every visiting ship of merchants, pirate hunters or corsairs causes a small local festival.

You certainly also know - and perhaps even tasted - the moon-sugar. A wondrous and dangerous substance that has been praised and vilified over the centuries, seen as the vice the khajiit are bringing or are infected with. The reality you can observe in Ne Quin-al is stranger than that. What we know as moon-sugar comes from the cash-crop plantations around the cities, selected primarily for the ease of cultivation and not for effect. And that is why the folk of Resdayn - or at least so I have been told - started distilling it into scooma for pure potency. The ranging clans of Ne Quin-al gather the wild cane instead, of the kind that grows slowly in the places almost sacred, and is slowly imbued with the properties of the land as it matures.

The spice that is produced from it doesn’t just allow you to wander through the phantasms of your mind passively. It fuels the peculiar magical practice that lies on the border of Illusion and Alteration - changing the world around just so minutely, freeing it from the rigid laws of the Earthbones into more flowing, dream-like logic - entirely fitting, since the locals believe the crop grows on the places where the blood of Lorkhaj was spilled. The Khajiit routinely use those effects in daily life, and that makes the travel through Ne-Quin’al so uncanny once you notice it - the tents of the sand clans are bigger on the inside than they should be, the narrow streets of the dusty cities open up into hidden multi-level quarters that shouldn’t fit there according to the plans, two khajiit going by the same path may end up in different destinations, and your companions who rush ahead may catch up with you from behind.
